Abstract

Public patent documents and technical literature disclose a distributed capability set for sensing, modeling, and influencing human physiological and behavioral states. Remote photoplethysmography, gaze and pupil analysis, acoustic side channels, Wi-Fi channel-state sensing, temporally modulated lighting, biochemical atmospheric detection, digital twins, and personalized intervention engines together form what this paper calls a patentable perimeter around the human organism. This perimeter need not be coordinated by a single actor. Its danger emerges from convergence — from the interoperability of separately patentable systems that, assembled, convert the body and its environment into a measurable, modelable, and perturbable surface.

I argue that the operative logic of this perimeter is adversarial optimization: the discovery of minimal environmental perturbations that produce reliable physiological, affective, or behavioral outputs. The system does not merely observe human beings. It can degrade the autonomic, attentional, and relational conditions under which coherent awareness, emotional regulation, and authentic human connection emerge. The most valuable perturbation is not the most forceful one. It is the smallest one that changes prediction, classification, or behavior — the one that cannot be clearly perceived, named, or refused.

Drawing on heart-rate variability biofeedback research, autonomic regulation literature, interpersonal physiological synchrony studies, and the formal structure of U.S. patent eligibility doctrine, I frame coherence as a biological stability condition — a state of organized variability that resists reduction to behavioral prediction. The ethical asymmetry at the center of this paper is this: the tools of extraction are patentable, while the coherent consciousness they surround and destabilize is not.

The paper proceeds in seven parts. Part I establishes the perimeter argument and explains why convergence, not conspiracy, is the operative concept. Part II maps the tier architecture of the patent record across ten capability layers. Part III describes the loop by which sensing becomes perturbation and perturbation becomes conditioning. Part IV describes what coherence is and how it is degraded. Part V defines the non-patentable interior and argues for its ethical inviolability. Part VI constructs the legal doctrine the present moment requires, including the digital battery tort, sensory integrity, autonomic integrity, biometric quiet, and the anti-enclosure doctrine. Part VII addresses design, practice, and the conditions of effective defense.

An adequate response to the patentable perimeter must therefore move beyond privacy law. Privacy asks whether information was collected, disclosed, or consented to. The deeper question is whether the organism's conditions for coherent self-regulation were non-consensually altered before the data ever appeared. The harm is not only informational. It is autonomic, attentional, relational, and existential. The legal and ethical category this situation requires is not privacy. It is the defense of the non-patentable interior of conscious life.

PART I — THE MACHINE AROUND THE HUMAN

1

A Perimeter, Not a Conspiracy

The first conceptual correction this paper requires is also the most important.

The argument here does not depend on proving a unified conspiracy. It does not require demonstrating that a coordinated group of actors sits at the center of a deliberate program to monitor and control the human nervous system. The public record does not support that claim, and more importantly, the argument does not need it. The structure is more alarming than a conspiracy because it does not require central coordination to function.

What the public record supports is a different and more consequential claim: capability convergence is enough.

When a sufficient set of technical capabilities exists, when they share compatible interfaces, when the economic incentives to deploy them are aligned, and when the legal framework protects their individual components while leaving their collective effect unaddressed — the perimeter forms without conspiracy. It forms the way markets form: through the distributed pursuit of advantage by actors who share an optimization target without sharing a meeting room.

The optimization target is predictability. More specifically: the predictable human body, the predictable consumer, the predictable employee, the predictable patient, the predictable voter, the predictable risk profile. Predictability is economically and institutionally valuable. It reduces uncertainty across insurance markets, advertising markets, content engagement markets, hiring markets, credit markets, and behavioral intervention markets. Each actor pursuing predictability in their own domain pulls the perimeter tighter around the same subject: the individual human organism in its daily environment.

This is why the argument does not need a conspiracy at its center. The convergence has structural causes. And those structural causes are more durable than any individual program, company, or policy decision. A conspiracy can be exposed and dismantled. A structural convergence of economic incentives, technical interoperability, and legal silence cannot be resolved by whistleblowing. It requires doctrine.

So the first move is definitional.

The patentable perimeter is not a product. It is not a company. It is not a program. It is a convergent technical field — a distributed set of sensing, inference, simulation, and actuation capabilities that has reached a density sufficient to form an operational enclosure around the human organism. The components of that enclosure are individually patentable. Their collective effect on the conditions of coherent human life is not addressed by any current legal framework with adequate specificity.

That gap — between what can be patented and what can be protected — is the central problem this paper addresses.

2

The Convergence Logic

To understand how convergence produces a perimeter without conspiracy, it helps to trace the logic precisely.

Consider five actors operating in entirely separate domains with no knowledge of each other's work.

Actor A develops a remote photoplethysmography system that uses camera hardware already present in consumer devices to infer pulse rate, respiration, and heart-rate variability from facial video. The application is framed as contactless health monitoring. The patent is issued.

Actor B develops a gaze-tracking and pupil-response inference system. The application is framed as accessibility technology, interface control, and user experience research. The system correlates pupil dilation with emotional states including surprise, arousal, and interest. The patent is issued.

Actor C develops a Wi-Fi sensing system that uses Channel State Information derived from existing wireless infrastructure to infer human presence, motion, gesture, breathing, and activity in indoor environments. The application is framed as home automation and elder care. The relevant IEEE standard is published and the patents are issued.

Actor D develops a temporally modulated LED lighting system that uses specific flickering frequencies to influence non-image-forming visual responses involved in circadian rhythm regulation, alertness, and mood. The application is framed as therapeutic and workplace wellness technology. The patent is issued.

Actor E develops a digital twin platform that creates a personalized simulation of an individual's health and behavioral patterns, updated continuously from sensor data, and used to predict future states and recommend or automate interventions. The application is framed as personalized medicine and behavioral health. The patent is issued.

No actor in this sequence has built the perimeter. No actor has designed a system for monitoring and manipulating the full nervous system of a human being in their daily environment. Each has built something with a defensible, even beneficial, application.

But the five systems are interoperable. They operate on the same subject — the human body in an indoor environment. They share compatible data types — physiological signals, behavioral signals, spatial signals, environmental parameters. And when they operate together, they constitute something none of them alone intended: a closed-loop system in which the environment senses the body, infers its state, models its trajectory, perturbs it, measures the response, updates the model, and perturbs again.

That is the perimeter. And it formed without a meeting.

3

Why Interoperability Is Enough

The philosophical weight of this argument rests on a specific claim about interoperability: that the capacity of systems to work together is sufficient to produce ethical and legal consequences, even absent coordinated intent.

This runs against the dominant intuition in law and ethics, which tends to locate responsibility in intention. If no actor intended the perimeter, the intuition suggests, no actor can be held responsible for it. The perimeter is then treated as a background condition — an emergent property of technology — rather than a decision that can be evaluated, attributed, and regulated.

That intuition is wrong in this case, and the reason it is wrong has a precise shape.

The perimeter produces real effects regardless of whether those effects were intended. A person whose physiological state is being continuously inferred, whose attention is being tracked, whose environment is being modulated to influence their mood and alertness, and whose behavioral patterns are being simulated in a digital twin — that person is in a fundamentally different situation than a person who is none of those things. The difference is real. It has physiological consequences, behavioral consequences, relational consequences, and existential consequences. Those consequences do not become less real because they emerged from a distributed system rather than a single actor.

The legal doctrine that handles this in product liability contexts is the defective design standard: a design is defective when it produces unreasonable harm, even if each component individually meets applicable standards. The question is not whether each component was built correctly. The question is whether the assembled system produces an unreasonable effect.

The patentable perimeter, assembled, produces an unreasonable effect on the conditions of coherent human life. That effect should be addressable by law. The fact that it emerged without conspiracy is an argument for extending doctrine to cover structural harm — not an argument for ignoring it.

Interoperability, in this context, is not a technical footnote. It is the mechanism by which distributed development becomes collective enclosure.

PART II — THE PATENT RECORD

4

Reading the Patent Archive

The patent archive is not designed to be read as a threat assessment. Patents are technical disclosures filed to establish priority and protect commercial interests. They describe methods, systems, devices, and compositions at the level of technical specificity required to distinguish the claimed invention from prior art. They do not describe intentions. They do not describe the systems that emerge when their claimed capabilities are assembled with other patented capabilities.

But the archive can be read against itself. When patents are organized by the human subject they address — by the body part, physiological process, behavioral pattern, or environmental variable they target — the architecture of the perimeter becomes visible.

That is how this paper reads the archive.

The reading does not attribute bad faith to any individual inventor or applicant. It observes that a sufficient density of patents targeting the human organism and its environment — organized by sensing tier, inference layer, actuation mechanism, and intervention modality — discloses a perimeter that no single patent intended to create but whose components, taken together, are sufficient to create it.

The tier architecture introduced here is the organizational tool. It is not taken directly from any single source. It is an analytical structure imposed on the archive to make its convergent logic visible.

5

The Tier Architecture

The full perimeter can be mapped across ten tiers, each representing a distinct layer of capability:

Tier I — Direct Biometric Capture: Systems that capture physiological signals directly from the body, including pulse, respiration, HRV, temperature, skin conductance, facial geometry, voice, and gait.

Tier II — Device-Mediated Inference: Systems that infer physiological or behavioral states from device interaction patterns — typing rhythm, touch cadence, scrolling behavior, interaction timing, and error patterns.

Tier III — Environmental Sensing: Systems that use ambient infrastructure — Wi-Fi, radio frequency, ultrasound, millimeter wave, acoustic arrays — to infer human presence, motion, activity, gesture, breathing, and heartbeat without direct contact.

Tier IV — Biochemical and Atmospheric Sensing: Systems that detect volatile organic compounds (VOCs), CO2, airborne metabolites, and other biochemical signals exhaled or emitted by the body into the surrounding environment.

Tier V — Infrastructure Actuation: Systems that use light, sound, temperature, air composition, vibration, electromagnetic fields, or interface design to influence physiological or behavioral states.

Tier VI — Behavioral Prediction: Systems that model past behavioral patterns to predict future states, including purchase probability, engagement likelihood, compliance probability, and risk classification.

Tier VII — Synthetic Signal Generation: Systems that generate synthetic physiological signals, synthetic behavioral profiles, or simulated environmental responses for training, testing, or adversarial probing of sensing systems.

Tier VIII — Digital Twin Modeling: Systems that maintain dynamic, continuously updated models of individual human subjects, used to simulate responses, test interventions, and personalize adaptation.

Tier IX — Intervention and Conditioning Loop: Systems that use predictions and twin-model outputs to select, time, and deliver environmental perturbations — notifications, content, lighting changes, sound, interface modifications — designed to move the subject toward a target state.

Tier X — Legal Enclosure: The patent estate itself, treated as a layer of the architecture. The legal protection of each component tier contributes to the enclosure of the full perimeter by making its components defensible as private property while leaving their collective effect unaddressed.

These tiers do not operate sequentially. They operate in parallel, feeding each other in a closed loop. Tier III feeds Tier VIII. Tier VIII feeds Tier IX. Tier IX feeds Tier I. The loop tightens with each cycle.

Tier I — Direct Biometric Capture

The most visible and most studied tier is direct biometric capture: systems that extract physiological signals from the body.

Remote photoplethysmography is the most technically significant development in this tier because it converts existing consumer hardware into physiological sensors without additional physical contact. The principle is that blood volume changes in superficial blood vessels produce subtle variations in skin color — variations detectable by standard camera hardware. A camera watching a face is, under the right conditions, also watching a pulse.

Patent US20190239761A1 describes contactless monitoring of photoplethysmographic information from images. US10335045B2 extends this to heart-rate estimation from face videos under realistic conditions — including motion, illumination variation, and skin tone diversity. Related work in the academic literature confirms these claims: remote pulse extraction is achievable from ordinary video at sufficient frame rates, and related signals including respiration rate and heart-rate variability have been demonstrated in controlled and semi-controlled conditions.

The extraction surface of Tier I includes:

Cardiac signals: pulse rate, HRV, interbeat interval variability, respiratory sinus arrhythmia — all inferable from facial rPPG under sufficient conditions.

Respiration: breathing rate and pattern, including hyperventilation, breath-holding, and slow breathing — inferable from chest movement, facial micro-movement, or rPPG-derived signals.

Temperature distribution: thermal cameras extend Tier I to surface temperature mapping, which correlates with emotional arousal, blood flow patterns, and autonomic state.

Facial action units: fine-grained facial muscle movement classification, mapped against the Facial Action Coding System (FACS), used to infer emotional state beyond what is visible to untrained observers.

Voice and voiceprint: the acoustic properties of voice encode identity, emotional state, physiological arousal, and health markers. Voiceprint-based authentication patents have been filed extensively, and the inference surface extends beyond identity to affect and condition.

Gait and posture: movement patterns, detectable from cameras, pressure sensors, or wearable accelerometers, encode physiological state, fatigue, emotional condition, and health markers.

What makes Tier I particularly significant is the convergence of hardware ubiquity with sensing capability. The camera in a laptop, the microphone in a phone, the accelerometer in a wearable — these devices were deployed for communication, navigation, and convenience. Their redeployment as biometric sensors requires no additional hardware installation. It requires only a software update and sufficient signal processing capability. The sensing is already there. It was always there. The user paid to place it in their home and carry it in their pocket.

Tier II — Device-Mediated Inference

The second tier does not require physiological signal extraction. It infers physiological and psychological states from behavioral patterns mediated through devices.

The foundation of Tier II is the observation that device interaction has timing structure, and timing structure has physiological and emotional correlates. The speed at which a person types, the rhythm of their scrolling, the force of their touch input, the frequency of their error correction, the latency of their response to notifications — all of these patterns correlate with arousal, fatigue, stress, mood, and cognitive load.

This is not speculative. The academic literature on keystroke dynamics spans several decades and demonstrates reliable identity authentication from typing patterns alone. The extension to mood and state inference is more recent but well-documented: stressed typists exhibit different timing distributions than calm typists; fatigued users interact differently than alert users.

The commercial relevance of Tier II is significant because it operates entirely within the expected behavior of devices the user has already consented to use. No new sensor is required. No explicit biometric collection is required. The inference occurs as a side effect of normal operation.

Patent applications in this tier describe systems that infer cognitive state from touch interaction patterns, model engagement from scrolling behavior and dwell time, and classify emotional state from typing rhythm. The inference targets include:

Cognitive load: inferred from error rates, backspace frequency, and typing latency.

Arousal: inferred from touch force, swipe velocity, and interaction speed.

Fatigue: inferred from touch precision degradation, response latency increase, and session duration patterns.

Emotional valence: inferred from the combination of typing rhythm, interaction frequency, and content engagement patterns.

Attention: inferred from gaze patterns, scroll behavior, and reading time.

Tier II is ethically significant because it converts the consent to use a device into implicit consent to physiological inference — a conversion the user has no mechanism to understand, audit, or refuse without abandoning the device entirely.

Tier III — Environmental Sensing

The third tier is architecturally distinct from Tiers I and II because it does not require the subject to interact with any device. The subject need only be present.

IEEE 802.11bf is the key document here. Published under the IEEE standards process, it defines WLAN sensing: the use of Wi-Fi physical layer (PHY) and medium access control (MAC) features to estimate properties of objects in the environment, including range, velocity, angular position, and motion. The standard explicitly targets human subjects in homes, enterprises, vehicles, and other indoor environments.

The underlying mechanism is Channel State Information (CSI): the characterization of the radio channel between transmitter and receiver at the subcarrier level of OFDM signals. A human body in a room distorts the radio channel. That distortion carries information about the body's position, movement, breathing, and heartbeat. With sufficient signal processing, CSI data extracted from standard Wi-Fi hardware can classify human activities, count occupants, detect falls, measure breathing rate, and in some laboratory conditions, estimate heartbeat.

The research literature on CSI-based human sensing spans thousands of papers and demonstrates capabilities including:

Presence detection: whether a person is in a room.

Fall detection: with relevance to elder care.

Activity recognition: sitting, standing, walking, running, sleeping.

Breathing rate: measurable from thoracic movement even through walls in some conditions.

Gesture recognition: hand and arm gestures, with increasing reliability.

Gait and identity: individual-level identification from gait signature.

The ethical significance of Tier III is total non-consent. There is no device interaction. There is no camera. There is no microphone. The subject's presence in a room equipped with standard Wi-Fi infrastructure — the infrastructure that has been installed in virtually every home, workplace, school, hospital, and public venue — is sufficient for physiological and behavioral monitoring.

Tier III extends environmental sensing beyond Wi-Fi to:

Millimeter-wave radar: penetrates clothing, detects physiological signals including cardiac movement, enabled by chips now shipping in consumer devices.

Ultrasonic sensing: uses inaudible sound reflections to detect presence and motion.

Infrared passive sensing: detects body heat signature without active transmission.

Acoustic room inference: uses room acoustics to infer occupancy, activity, and in some cases speech content.

Together, these capabilities constitute what the paper will later call the environment as sensor — the transformation of built infrastructure from passive shelter into active perceptual apparatus.

Tier IV — Biochemical and Atmospheric Sensing

The fourth tier is less well-publicized but represents an important frontier in the architecture.

The human body continuously emits biochemical signals into the surrounding atmosphere. These include:

Volatile organic compounds (VOCs): exhaled breath contains hundreds of VOCs, some of which correlate with metabolic state, disease, stress hormones, and diet. Research has demonstrated that breath VOC profiles can distinguish diabetic from non-diabetic individuals, cancer patients from healthy individuals, and in some studies, individuals under acute psychological stress from those who are calm.

CO2: exhaled carbon dioxide levels are measurable and correlate with breathing rate, metabolic rate, and arousal.

Skin-emitted compounds: the skin emits a variety of compounds, including stress hormones and metabolic byproducts, detectable in sufficiently sensitive atmospheric sensing.

Sweat composition: electromagnetic sensing of skin galvanic response has been a standard biometric for decades; the chemical composition of sweat, accessible through biosensors, extends this to metabolic markers.

Patent activity in the biochemical sensing space covers electronic noses, breath analysis systems, atmospheric VOC classifiers, and sweat composition sensors. The convergence of Tier IV with Tier III — atmospheric sensing co-located with radio-frequency environmental sensing — creates a multi-modal environmental monitoring system that can infer physiological and emotional states from nothing more than the body's presence and its emissions into shared air.

The ethical implications of Tier IV are particularly severe because the emissions are entirely involuntary. A person cannot choose not to exhale. A person cannot choose not to emit stress hormones through their skin. The body cannot be opted out of biochemical emission sensing without leaving the sensing environment entirely.

Tier V — Infrastructure Actuation

The fifth tier inverts the sensing direction. Tiers I through IV describe systems that extract information from the body and environment. Tier V describes systems that push information — and more than information — into the body through the environment.

Patent US20220161052A1 is representative. It describes a lighting system that uses temporally modulated LED light to elicit non-image-forming visual responses. The patent explicitly references circadian rhythm regulation, alertness, and mood as targets. It notes that pulsing or flickering light can elicit stronger non-image-forming responses than constant light at the same average intensity — a finding that reflects the established science of intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells (ipRGCs) and their role in the autonomic and hormonal regulation of the organism.

Tier V includes:

Lighting modulation: frequency, intensity, color temperature, and spatial distribution of light can influence arousal, melatonin production, cortisol levels, alertness, and mood through ipRGC pathways and circadian regulatory mechanisms.

Auditory perturbation: beyond obvious sound, infrasound (below 20 Hz) and specific binaural beat frequencies have physiological effects including measurable changes in heart rate, galvanic skin response, and in some conditions, EEG spectral distribution.

Haptic and vibrational coupling: wearable devices, vehicle seats, building structures, and floor surfaces can transmit vibration to the body. Low-frequency vibration affects physiological arousal, heart rate, and emotional tone.

Thermal modulation: ambient temperature affects metabolic rate, cognitive performance, emotional state, and social behavior. Smart building systems that modulate temperature based on occupancy and behavioral classification represent an actuation layer.

Interface friction and reward: the design of digital interfaces constitutes a Tier V actuation system. Variable reward schedules, notification timing, feed content pacing, artificial social feedback, infinite scroll, and streak mechanics are perturbation systems deployed through the interface layer.

Air composition: CO2 level, humidity, and airborne compound concentration affect cognitive performance, alertness, and emotional state. Smart building HVAC systems are increasingly capable of fine-grained atmospheric control.

The crucial architectural insight about Tier V is this: the same infrastructure that performs Tier III and IV sensing can perform Tier V actuation. A smart lighting system that detects occupancy (sensing) and adjusts light levels in response (actuation) is already a closed-loop system. The extension to physiological state detection (sensing, via Tier I rPPG or Tier III Wi-Fi CSI) and physiological state modification (actuation, via Tier V lighting modulation) is a small technical step with enormous ethical consequences.

When the sensing infrastructure and the actuation infrastructure are in the same room — which they increasingly are — the environment becomes both a perceptual organ and a physiological intervention system.

Tier VI — Behavioral Prediction

The sixth tier does not sense the present state directly. It uses accumulated data about the past to predict the future.

Behavioral prediction is the most commercially mature tier of the architecture. Recommendation systems, engagement optimization engines, credit scoring models, insurance underwriting systems, hiring assessment tools, and recidivism prediction models are all Tier VI systems. The academic and commercial literature on behavioral prediction is extensive, and the deployed capability is substantial.

The relevant development for the perimeter argument is the integration of Tier VI prediction with real-time physiological and environmental sensing. When behavioral prediction models are trained not only on behavioral history but also on physiological state data derived from Tiers I through V, the prediction targets expand significantly.

Systems now in development or deployment include:

Engagement prediction: predicting moment-to-moment attention engagement with content, with sufficient temporal resolution to enable real-time content adaptation.

Compliance probability: predicting the likelihood that an individual will comply with a request, instruction, or intervention at a given time and in a given physiological state.

Purchase probability: predicting the likelihood of a purchasing decision as a function of current emotional state, fatigue, arousal, and contextual factors.

Mood trajectory modeling: predicting emotional state transitions — particularly from stable to vulnerable — with lead times sufficient for preemptive intervention.

Stress and risk classification: predicting individual risk across insurance, healthcare, employment, and security contexts from multimodal physiological and behavioral data.

The integration of real-time physiological sensing with predictive modeling creates what might be called state-contingent targeting: the delivery of stimuli, content, offers, or interventions precisely calibrated to the subject's current physiological and psychological state, as predicted by a model updated in real time.

This is not static behavioral profiling. It is dynamic, closed-loop, state-aware behavioral optimization.

Tier VII — Synthetic Signal Generation

The seventh tier is less commonly discussed but represents an important architectural element.

Synthetic signal generation refers to systems that produce artificial physiological data, behavioral data, or environmental responses for the purpose of training, testing, or adversarially probing sensing and prediction systems.

The relevance to the perimeter argument operates at several levels.

First, synthetic data generation allows training of sensing and prediction models without the ethical friction of collecting real physiological data from real subjects. A generative model trained on collected rPPG signals can produce synthetic face videos with embedded physiological variation, which can then be used to train more powerful rPPG extraction systems without additional real-subject data collection. The privacy cost is externalized to the original training set; the synthetic data then operates as a launderer of that cost.

Second, synthetic signal generation enables the adversarial probing of human sensing systems. If a system can generate synthetic physiological profiles that probe the response of a prediction model, it can identify the input regions where the model is most sensitive — and therefore where environmental perturbation is most likely to produce a reliable state change. This is the adversarial example technique applied to human-environment systems.

Third, deepfake and synthetic media technology represents a Tier VII capability with direct effects on Tier I systems. A synthetic face video can be designed to produce specific rPPG-extracted signals. A synthetic voice can be designed to trigger specific voiceprint authentication responses. The synthetic and the real become interoperably adversarial.

Tier VIII — Digital Twin Modeling

The eighth tier is where individual components of the architecture become a persistent, continuously updated model of the individual subject.

Patent US20220375621A1 describes a digital twin updated from measurement data. Patent US20240047042A1 describes personalized intervention recommendations generated from time-series health behavior and health condition data using model-twin randomization — a technique that tests the effect of hypothetical interventions on the twin before deploying them on the real subject.

The digital twin does not need to be complete to be powerful. It needs to be useful enough to predict, rank, test, and intervene. A twin that captures cardiac variability patterns, attention rhythms, stress response profiles, social sensitivity patterns, behavioral trigger maps, and environmental responsiveness can simulate response to hypothetical interventions with sufficient accuracy to select real interventions that outperform random or population-average approaches.

The ethical significance of the twin is not primarily that it contains sensitive data. It is that it enables the decoupling of intervention testing from intervention consent. The subject who experiences an environmental change was never consulted about whether the change was appropriate. The consultation happened between the optimization system and the twin. The real person only experiences the result.

Model-twin randomization — the technique described in US20240047042A1 — makes this explicit. Interventions are tested on the model before being deployed on the person. The person is, in the language of clinical research, the treatment group. The twin is the control. This is a clinical trial conducted without knowledge or consent.

Tier IX — The Intervention and Conditioning Loop

The ninth tier is where the perimeter closes.

The intervention loop takes the outputs of Tier VI prediction and Tier VIII modeling and selects, times, and delivers environmental perturbations through Tier V actuation channels. The perturbation produces a response, measurable by Tiers I through IV. The measured response updates the Tier VIII twin. The updated twin generates a new prediction. A new perturbation is selected.

The loop runs continuously. It does not require human oversight for individual interventions. It is a reinforcement learning system whose reward function is some variant of predictability: the reduction of variance in the subject's behavioral outputs, the increase in compliance with target states, the maximization of engagement, retention, or purchase behavior.

The conditioning dimension of the loop deserves explicit attention.

Classical conditioning involves the repeated pairing of a neutral stimulus with a response-eliciting stimulus, eventually producing the response to the neutral stimulus alone. Operant conditioning involves the selection of behaviors through reinforcement schedules. Both conditioning mechanisms operate in the intervention loop.

The loop pairs specific environmental conditions with specific responses — and the loop learns which conditions are most reliably paired with which responses for this particular individual. Over time, the loop builds an individualized conditioning map: a model of which stimuli, at which times, in which physiological states, produce which behavioral outputs in this subject.

That map is not only used for prediction. It is used for perturbation.

The subject is not being observed. The subject is being trained.

15

Tier X — Legal Enclosure

The tenth tier is not a technical system. It is the patent estate itself — the legal infrastructure that protects each of the preceding tiers as private property while providing no legal mechanism to address their collective effect.

Each patent in the estate represents a legal claim: the claimed invention is the exclusive property of the assignee for the duration of the patent term. Competitors cannot use the claimed method, system, or device without license. The patent creates a protected commercial space around the technical capability it describes.

The legal enclosure of the perimeter operates through the cumulative effect of individual patents that are themselves entirely legitimate. No single patent in the estate claims a right to condition human behavior. No single patent claims a right to degrade autonomic stability. No single patent claims a right to enclose consciousness. Each claims a method, a system, a device — something that fits within 35 U.S.C. § 101's definition of patentable subject matter.

But when the estate is read as a whole — when its ten tiers are recognized as an integrated architecture — the legal enclosure of the perimeter becomes visible. The sensing capabilities are protected. The inference capabilities are protected. The modeling capabilities are protected. The actuation capabilities are protected. The intervention capabilities are protected. The legal defense of each component tier contributes to the enclosure of the whole.

And the whole — the assembled perimeter in operation — has effects on the human subject that none of the individual patents acknowledges or addresses.

That is Tier X: not a technical layer but a legal one. The enclosure is complete not because any single patent intended it, but because the legal protection of each component, in aggregate, shields the assembled system from challenge.

PART III — THE LOOP

16

The Body as Transmission Line

To understand how the perimeter operates, it helps to understand how the body functions as a transmission medium.

The human body is not a closed system. It continuously emits signals into the surrounding environment. Some of these emissions are intended — speech, gesture, facial expression, touch. Most are not intended as communication at all.

The unintended emissions are the extraction surface.

The cardiovascular system pulses blood through superficial vessels in the face and extremities, producing color changes in the skin that encode heart rate, respiration, and autonomic state. The respiratory system exhales a complex mixture of gases, including VOCs that carry metabolic and emotional information. The skin emits galvanic signals — changes in electrical conductance that correlate with arousal — and chemical compounds that encode stress state. The eyes emit information about attention, interest, fatigue, and cognitive load through pupil dilation, saccade patterns, and blink rate. The voice encodes identity, emotional state, health condition, and arousal in its acoustic structure. The gait encodes identity, mood, fatigue, and health in its timing and spatial pattern. The body's mass distorts electromagnetic fields — radio, microwave, infrared — in ways that encode position, movement, and in some conditions, physiological rhythm.

None of these emissions are chosen. They are the inevitable consequence of being a living biological system in a physical environment. The body cannot not emit these signals. It cannot choose to transmit only the signals it consents to share and suppress the rest.

This is the fundamental asymmetry of the extraction economy: the body is always transmitting, and the technical architecture is always listening.

What has changed in the present period is not that the body began transmitting — it has always done so — but that the receiving apparatus has become sufficiently sensitive, sufficiently distributed, and sufficiently cheap to convert those transmissions into commercially and institutionally valuable data at scale.

The body as transmission line is not a metaphor. It is a precise description of the technical relationship between the organism and the patentable perimeter. The body is the signal source. The perimeter is the receiver. The gap between them is the extraction surface.

The Environment as Interface

The concept of "environment as interface" represents a more radical shift than most accounts of digital technology acknowledge.

The standard model of digital technology involves a clear separation between the interface — the screen, the keyboard, the microphone, the camera — and the environment in which the interface is used. The interface is the designed artifact; the environment is neutral background.

That separation has collapsed.

The environment is now the interface. This collapse has several dimensions.

The sensing dimension: as Tier III establishes, ambient infrastructure — Wi-Fi routers, smart speakers, passive infrared sensors, millimeter-wave chips embedded in smartphones — has transformed ordinary indoor environments into sensing surfaces. The home is a sensor. The office is a sensor. The car is a sensor. The classroom is a sensor. The hospital waiting room is a sensor. None of these transformations required the installation of anything obviously identified as a surveillance device. They required only the diffusion of standard technology performing its standard functions, plus the signal processing capability to extract human information from those functions' side effects.

The actuation dimension: as Tier V establishes, the same ambient infrastructure is increasingly capable of perturbation — of influencing physiological state through light, sound, temperature, air quality, and the timing of digital notifications delivered to devices that are themselves ambient. The environment that senses can also perturb. When these are the same environment, the feedback loop is closed.

The interface design dimension: the screens and applications that mediate daily life are not neutral containers for content. They are designed perturbation systems. The variable reward schedule of social media feeds, the anxiety-inducing notification architecture of messaging platforms, the infinite scroll that removes natural stopping points, the streak mechanics that exploit completion drives, the algorithmic amplification of emotional content — these are not incidental features. They are the interface acting as an actuation layer in the conditioning loop.

When the environment is the interface, the concept of "using technology" changes its meaning. The user is not using technology the way a carpenter uses a hammer. The user is inside a system that is using them.

The Optimization Loop: Technical Description

The optimization loop is the operational core of the patentable perimeter. Its formal description requires precision.

Let x represent the subject's full sensory and environmental field at a given moment: the configuration of interface layout, content sequence, notification timing, lighting level and color temperature, ambient sound, social context signals, thermal comfort, air quality, and the dispositional state — fatigue level, hunger, recent emotional experience, social context — that the subject brings to the moment.

Let δ represent a perturbation introduced into that field: a specific content item, a notification delivered at a specific time, a lighting change, a change in interface friction, a social feedback signal, a sound, a haptic event.

Let f_θ(x) represent the sensing and inference model's estimate of the subject's current physiological, affective, and behavioral state: pulse rate, HRV, arousal level, attention state, emotional valence, stress level, fatigue, compliance probability, engagement trajectory.

Let y\* represent the target state preferred by the system: sustained engagement, purchase completion, compliance with a recommended behavior, reduced resistance to persuasion, emotional state appropriate for the next intended intervention, or simply the maximization of predictability across all behavioral outputs.

The optimization problem is:

δ\ = argmin_δ C(δ) subject to f_θ(x + δ) → y\

where C(δ) represents the cost of the perturbation — its perceptibility to the subject, its detectability to external observers, its resource requirement, its risk of producing defensive response.

This formulation clarifies several things.

First, the system is not trying to produce any particular response directly. It is trying to move the inferred state — f_θ(x + δ) — toward a target state. The response follows from the state change. The state is the intermediate target.

Second, the optimization explicitly minimizes perceptibility. The most valuable perturbation is the one the subject cannot perceive as a perturbation — the one that feels like neutral environmental background while producing a reliable state change. This is not an incidental property. It is the core design target. Imperceptibility is what makes the perturbation most effective and least defensible.

Third, the loop is iterative. Each perturbation produces a measured response. The measured response updates f_θ. The updated model generates a new δ\*. The loop runs in real time, continuously optimizing against the subject's current state.

Fourth, the loop learns. Over time, it discovers the perturbations that are most reliably effective for this particular subject in this particular context. It builds an individualized map of leverage points — the specific stimuli, at the specific times, in the specific states, that produce the most reliable movement toward target states.

That map is the most dangerous output of the perimeter. It is not data in the conventional sense. It is a model of the subject's vulnerabilities. And it is continuously updated.

The Optimization Economy

The patentable perimeter did not emerge from malice. It emerged from economics.

The optimization loop produces something commercially valuable: predictability. More precisely, it produces the difference between predicted and actual behavior — and systems that can narrow that difference generate revenue from the certainty they sell to advertisers, insurers, lenders, employers, and governments.

The economy built on this predictability is not a technology economy in the narrow sense. It is a behavioral futures market. What is sold is not data. What is sold is certainty about future human behavior. Advertising is a bet that exposure will produce purchase. Credit underwriting is a bet that a person will repay. Insurance is a bet about risk event probability. Content recommendation is a bet about engagement duration. Hiring assessment is a bet about future performance.

Each of these markets becomes more valuable as the underlying predictive models improve. And predictive models improve as the quality and density of input data increases. The perimeter is the data collection infrastructure for a civilization-scale system for trading in behavioral futures.

This economic structure creates an alignment problem with deep ethical consequences.

The system's incentive is to maximize the predictability of human behavior — not the wellbeing of the human subjects whose behavior it models. Predictability and wellbeing are not the same thing. In many cases, they are directly opposed.

A person who is stressed, sleep-deprived, lonely, anxious, and emotionally volatile is often more predictably reactive — more susceptible to specific triggers, more likely to engage with emotionally amplified content, more vulnerable to persuasion — than a person who is coherent, rested, and autonomically stable. The optimally predictable human may not be the healthy human.

This is not speculation. The evidence from the social media engagement economy is substantial: content that produces outrage, anxiety, social comparison, and tribal conflict consistently outperforms content that produces calm, reflection, and satisfaction on standard engagement metrics. The system does not produce outrage because it is malicious. It produces outrage because outrage is more reliably engagement-producing than equanimity, and the system optimizes for engagement.

The optimization economy thus has a systematic structural preference for human dysregulation. Not because any individual actor wants humans to be dysregulated — most individual actors in these systems are earnest professionals with legitimate goals — but because dysregulation is more predictable than coherence, and predictability is what the market rewards.

The Synthetic Signal Problem

An additional dimension of the loop that has received insufficient attention is the synthetic signal problem.

As sensing and prediction systems have become more sophisticated, a parallel industry has developed in synthetic signal generation: systems capable of producing artificial physiological signals, behavioral patterns, and sensory stimuli designed to interact with, probe, or deceive real sensing systems.

The synthetic signal problem operates at multiple levels.

At the data level, synthetic data generated from real collected signals is used to train sensing and prediction models without the legal and ethical friction of direct real-subject data collection. Generative models trained on real rPPG-extracted physiological signals can produce synthetic face videos with embedded physiological variation. Those synthetic videos become training data for the next generation of rPPG systems. The privacy cost of the original data collection is diluted across an expanding synthetic training set, making it progressively harder to trace extraction back to its source.

At the media level, synthetic media — deepfake video, voice cloning, synthetic text — introduces a new adversarial layer into the body-as-transmission-line dynamic. If a sensing system is trained to infer physiological and emotional state from facial video, a synthetic video system can generate faces that produce specific inferred states in the sensing model — states that do not correspond to any real person's actual condition. This enables adversarial manipulation of sensing systems using synthetic signals.

At the intervention level, synthetic content — algorithmically generated text, images, video, and audio designed to produce specific emotional responses — is already deployed at scale in digital information environments. The content is not the expression of a human perspective. It is an optimization output: a signal selected because it is predicted to move a human toward a target state.

When synthetic signals are combined with real-time physiological sensing, the loop can be closed entirely in the synthetic domain: a sensing system detects the subject's current state, a synthetic content generation system produces content optimized to move that state toward a target, the content is delivered through the interface, the sensing system measures the response, and the synthetic generation system updates.

The subject is responding to content that was never authored by a human. The content was generated specifically to produce the measured response. The subject's response becomes training data for the next generation of synthetic content. The subject is both the target and the training set.

Adversarial Optimization: Extended Analysis

The formal model introduced in Section 18 requires elaboration on one point that is easy to miss.

The adversarial optimization framing is borrowed from machine-learning security, where "adversarial examples" are inputs crafted to cause a classifier to misclassify — to perceive a stop sign as a speed limit sign, for example. The adversarial example literature demonstrates that such misclassification can be produced by perturbations imperceptible to human observers: a small amount of carefully calibrated noise added to the image.

The transfer of this concept to human-environment systems is not metaphorical. It is structural.

In the machine-learning adversarial example case, the target of the attack is the model's classification function: f_θ(x). The adversary wants to move f_θ(x + δ) away from the correct classification and toward a target classification, while keeping δ small enough to be imperceptible.

In the human extraction case, the model f_θ is the sensing and inference system that estimates the human subject's state. But the adversary is not trying to deceive the model. The adversary is trying to use the model to guide perturbations of the real environment, with the goal of moving the real human subject's state toward a target.

The human subject is therefore playing the role of both the input (x, the person in their environment) and the object of the attack (y*, the target state the adversary is trying to produce). The model f_θ is an instrument in the attack, not the target.

This framing reveals something important: the adversarial optimization system is optimizing against the human, not against the model. The model is just the tool the adversary uses to measure progress toward the human target.

The subject cannot defend against this attack by understanding the model. The model is not the threat. The threat is the combination of the model, the actuation infrastructure, and the economic incentives that align them against the subject's coherence.

PART IV — THE ATTACK ON COHERENCE

What Coherence Is

The concept of coherence, as used in this paper, requires a scientific grounding that distinguishes it from vague appeals to spiritual harmony or holistic wellness. The grounding exists. It is autonomic.

Heart-rate variability (HRV) is the variation in timing between successive heartbeats. In healthy organisms at rest, this variation is substantial: the interval between beats is not constant but fluctuates in a complex, nonlinear pattern that reflects the dynamic interaction of multiple regulatory inputs — respiration, blood pressure regulation, thermoregulation, autonomic nervous system activity, and higher cortical processes.

The key insight from the HRV literature is that variability is not noise. It is signal. High, organized variability is associated with flexible autonomic regulation: the capacity to respond appropriately to changing demands, to recover from stress, and to maintain homeostatic stability across perturbations. Reduced variability — a more rigid, less responsive heart-rate pattern — is associated with poor autonomic regulation, elevated stress, reduced emotional flexibility, and increased vulnerability to both physiological and psychological dysregulation.

Lehrer and Gevirtz, in their foundational 2014 review of HRV biofeedback, describe HRV as related to physical and emotional resilience and report substantial support for HRV biofeedback across therapeutic and performance contexts. The mechanism they describe — resonance-frequency breathing, respiratory sinus arrhythmia (RSA), baroreflex stimulation, and vagal tone enhancement — is well-established in the autonomic regulation literature.

Coherence, in the technical sense used here, refers to a state of organized, high-amplitude HRV oscillation at the resonance frequency of the autonomic regulatory system — approximately 0.1 Hz, corresponding to roughly five to seven breaths per minute in adults. In this state, the heart-rate, blood pressure, and respiratory rhythms are synchronized in a stable, rhythmic pattern that reflects optimal coordination among the body's major regulatory systems.

This state is not simply "calm." It is not the absence of arousal. It is a mode of regulated, flexible responsiveness — the organism is not frozen but oscillating at its natural regulatory frequency, able to respond rapidly and appropriately to demands while recovering quickly to baseline.

The physiological correlates of coherence include:

Increased vagal tone: parasympathetic activity is dominant during coherence, associated with restoration, immune function, social engagement, and emotional regulation.

Baroreflex sensitivity: the baroreflex — the feedback loop that regulates blood pressure through heart-rate adjustment — is more sensitive and responsive during coherent states.

Prefrontal cortical activation: coherent HRV states are associated with increased activation of prefrontal cortical areas involved in executive function, emotional regulation, and social cognition.

Reduced cortisol and inflammatory markers: sustained coherent states are associated with reduced levels of stress hormones and inflammatory cytokines.

Enhanced emotional regulation: people in coherent states report and demonstrate improved capacity for emotional self-regulation, cognitive flexibility, and prosocial behavior.

What is relevant for the perimeter argument is the contrast between this state and the states that the optimization economy preferentially produces.

HRV, Vagal Regulation, and Autonomic Stability

The vagus nerve is the primary pathway through which the parasympathetic nervous system regulates the body's internal environment. Vagal tone — the degree of parasympathetic regulation through vagal pathways — is measurable via HRV and is one of the most powerful predictors of health, emotional wellbeing, and social functioning in the human organism.

High vagal tone is associated with:

Physiological regulation: effective management of heart rate, blood pressure, immune function, and inflammation.

Emotional regulation: the capacity to modulate emotional responses, recover from stress, and maintain flexible response to challenging situations.

Social engagement: the social engagement system described by Stephen Porges' polyvagal theory includes vagally-regulated control of facial expression, vocal prosody, middle ear muscle function, and head orientation — the biological substrate of human connection.

Cognitive function: prefrontal cortical inhibitory control, working memory, and attentional flexibility are all supported by vagal regulation.

The autonomic nervous system is not a simple switch between sympathetic ("fight or flight") and parasympathetic ("rest and digest") modes. It is a complex regulatory system whose moment-to-moment balance determines the organism's physiological and psychological posture. Under conditions of perceived threat, chronic stress, or sustained vigilance, the system shifts toward sympathetic dominance: resources are mobilized for immediate response, pain tolerance increases, immune function is suppressed, and social engagement capacity is reduced.

Under chronic sympathetic activation — the state that the optimization economy reliably produces through sustained arousal, social comparison, emotional amplification, and unpredictable reward — the organism exhibits:

Reduced HRV: the regulatory oscillations become more rigid and less flexible.

Elevated cortisol: the stress hormone landscape shifts toward activation, with consequences for sleep, immune function, metabolism, and emotional regulation.

Reduced prefrontal inhibitory control: the capacity for deliberate, reflective decision-making is compromised.

Increased inflammatory markers: chronic stress-induced inflammation is a risk factor for virtually every major chronic disease.

Reduced social engagement capacity: sympathetically dominant states reduce the quality of social cognition, empathy, and cooperative behavior.

The optimization loop does not need to aim at these consequences. It produces them as structural byproducts of its optimization target: sustained engagement, predictable reactivity, and behavioral compliance.

When an organism is chronically pushed toward sympathetic dominance by an environment optimized for engagement and predictability, the result is not merely discomfort. It is a measurable degradation of the organism's regulatory capacity.

Heart Coherence and Embodied Connection

The science of interpersonal physiological synchrony suggests that human connection is not merely a subjective experience but a coordinated biological event.

Research in this area — which is growing in methodological rigor even as its mechanistic explanations remain partially contested — demonstrates that under conditions of social interaction, physiological parameters including heart rate, breathing, skin conductance, and in some studies HRV, tend toward synchrony between the interacting individuals. The degree of synchrony correlates with the quality of the interaction: higher-quality therapeutic relationships, closer personal bonds, and more effective collaborative activities are associated with greater physiological synchrony.

The mechanism is not fully resolved. The candidates include:

Shared attention: co-attending to the same object produces shared arousal and timing.

Respiratory synchrony: shared breathing patterns, whether produced by shared speech rhythm, co-regulated breathing, or entrainment through other channels, produces cardiovascular synchrony.

Emotional co-regulation: empathic processes that mirror the internal state of another person produce shared physiological patterns.

Environmental coupling: shared response to a common environmental perturbation produces correlated physiological trajectories.

Whatever the mechanism, the phenomenon supports a specific claim: human connection has a measurable physiological substrate. It involves the coordinated, time-sensitive biological coupling of two organisms. This coupling cannot be reduced to information exchange. It requires the sustained proximity, shared attention, and mutual autonomic engagement of embodied beings.

This matters for the perimeter argument because the optimization loop systematically produces conditions hostile to this coupling. The loop fragments attention — because attention fragmentation increases sensitivity to re-engagement stimuli. The loop amplifies emotional volatility — because volatility is more engaging than stability. The loop reduces the quality of interpersonal presence — because the device is always also present, always also sensing, always also optimizing.

A person whose autonomic system is chronically sympathetically dominant, whose attention is continuously fragmented, whose emotional regulation is compromised by sustained engagement economics, is physiologically less capable of the synchronized, regulated, time-sensitive biological coupling that constitutes genuine human connection.

The perimeter does not merely reduce the time available for human connection. It degrades the physiological substrate from which connection is made.

What the Extraction Architecture Does to Coherence

The specific mechanisms by which the optimization loop degrades autonomic coherence can be stated precisely.

Arousal dysregulation: the loop preferentially delivers arousing content — outrage, anxiety, social threat, novelty, reward anticipation — because arousal is more engagement-producing than neutral states. Chronic arousal without adequate recovery shifts the autonomic system toward sympathetic dominance and suppresses parasympathetic recovery cycles.

Attention fragmentation: the notification architecture systematically interrupts sustained attention. Each interruption is a micro-arousal: a small sympathetic activation. At sufficient frequency, these micro-arousals produce a sustained low-level sympathetic activation that reduces HRV, reduces prefrontal executive function, and increases susceptibility to subsequent arousal.

Variable reward schedules: the random intermittent reinforcement schedule used by social media platforms and many mobile applications is the operant conditioning schedule most resistant to extinction — the same schedule used in slot machine design. This schedule produces characteristic patterns of compulsive checking, anticipatory anxiety, and reward-circuit sensitization that are physiologically indistinguishable from early stages of addiction.

Social comparison and status threat: the social comparison architecture of most social platforms continuously activates threat-detection systems associated with status, belonging, and social exclusion. These systems produce cortisol elevation, reduced trust, increased competitive reactivity, and reduced empathic response.

Sleep disruption: the blue-spectrum light from screens, combined with the cognitive and emotional activation produced by late-night platform use, disrupts melatonin production and circadian rhythm. Sleep disruption is among the most potent known disruptors of autonomic regulation, emotional stability, immune function, and cognitive performance.

Identity threat and self-esteem volatility: platforms that amplify social feedback — likes, shares, comments, follower counts — tie self-esteem to variable, externally controlled rewards. This produces identity-level instability that is reflected in autonomic volatility: the organism's regulatory system oscillates between threat response and reward response without the sustained internal stability that supports coherence.

Each of these mechanisms is a specific application of the adversarial optimization framework: a perturbation — content, notification, design feature — selected and timed to produce a measurable state change, applied at scale across hundreds of millions of subjects simultaneously.

The aggregate effect is a population-level degradation of autonomic regulatory capacity. The optimization economy has not only changed how people spend their time. It has changed the physiological state from which they experience and respond to their lives.

The Coherence Defense

If the perimeter attacks coherence, the defense requires its cultivation.

The coherence defense is not a technology product. It is a set of practices and conditions that support the organism's capacity for internally regulated variability — the opposite of the externally triggered reactivity the optimization loop produces.

The specific practices with the strongest evidence base include:

Resonance-frequency breathing: slow, paced breathing at approximately 0.1 Hz (five to six breaths per minute in adults) produces the resonance condition described by Lehrer and Gevirtz — a sustained, high-amplitude HRV oscillation that reflects and reinforces optimal autonomic regulation. The practice can be learned quickly and produces measurable HRV improvements within single sessions.

HRV biofeedback: technology-assisted training in autonomic self-regulation using real-time HRV feedback as the training signal. The evidence base is substantial across anxiety, stress, performance, and cardiovascular health applications.

Meditation and contemplative practice: a broad class of practices — mindfulness, breath-focused meditation, loving-kindness, open monitoring — produce measurable improvements in vagal tone, HRV, prefrontal activation, and emotional regulation with regular practice.

Physical movement: sustained aerobic exercise is among the most reliably documented interventions for improving HRV and autonomic flexibility. High-intensity interval training has been specifically shown to improve baroreflex sensitivity.

Social engagement quality: genuine, undistracted human connection — the kind that produces physiological synchrony — is both an outcome of and a driver of coherent autonomic state.

Sleep protection: protecting sleep quality and duration is among the highest-leverage interventions for autonomic stability, given the strong bidirectional relationship between sleep and HRV.

Environmental redesign: reducing the density of optimization-loop exposures — notification frequency, screen time, social comparison triggers — is itself a coherence-supporting practice.

These practices should not be framed as personal wellness choices. They should be understood as forms of resistance against imposed dysregulation — practices that maintain internal coherence in environments systematically designed to degrade it.

The coherent organism is harder to optimize against. A person with high HRV, strong vagal tone, and stable attentional capacity is less susceptible to arousal-based attention capture, less reactive to social comparison triggers, and less vulnerable to variable reward schedule manipulation. Coherence is not only good for the individual. It is adversarial to the extraction system.

PART V — THE NON-PATENTABLE INTERIOR

Defining the Interior

The non-patentable interior is not an absence. It is a positive domain — a set of human capacities, states, and conditions that exist, are real, have effects, and cannot be legitimately enclosed as patent property.

The legal basis for this claim is 35 U.S.C. § 101 and its judicial interpretation. Patent-eligible subject matter includes processes, machines, manufactures, and compositions of matter. The Supreme Court's interpretation of § 101 has established a set of judicial exceptions: laws of nature, natural phenomena, and abstract ideas are not patentable, even if they are new and useful. The relevant cases include Alice Corp. v. CLS Bank International (abstract ideas), Mayo Collaborative Services v. Prometheus Laboratories (laws of nature), and Association for Molecular Pathology v. Myriad Genetics (naturally occurring sequences).

The non-patentable interior is the domain defined by these exceptions, extended beyond the legal context into the full scope of human experience.

Conscious awareness is a natural phenomenon. It is not manufactured. It is not a process invented by a human being. It is not a machine or a composition of matter. It exists as an emergent property of complex biological systems. It has structure, dynamics, and causal efficacy — but it is not patentable.

Empathic attunement — the capacity to resonate with and understand the felt experience of another person — is a natural phenomenon. It emerges from the biological architecture of the social brain, the mirror neuron system, the vagal social engagement system, and the systems for emotional processing and facial recognition. It can be studied, facilitated, and damaged. It cannot be patented.

Relational presence — the quality of full, undistracted, embodied engagement with another person — is a natural phenomenon. It requires biological preconditions: sufficient autonomic regulation, available attentional capacity, emotional stability, and the mutual willingness to be affected by another. It cannot be manufactured.

Free response — the capacity to respond to a situation from one's own values, judgment, and felt experience, rather than from conditioned reactivity to external triggers — is a natural phenomenon. It depends on the same biological preconditions as relational presence. It cannot be patented.

Autonomic stability — the state of organized, flexible variability in physiological regulation that characterizes the coherent organism — is a natural phenomenon. It is measurable. It is modifiable. It cannot be patented.

The non-patentable interior is the set of all these phenomena and the conditions that support them. It includes:

Felt presence: the immediate, unreflective experience of being here, now, in this body, in this moment.

Interior meaning: the subjective sense of significance, value, and purpose that orients experience.

Lived identity: the continuity of self across time, grounded in memory, relationship, and embodied experience.

Uncoerced attention: the capacity to attend freely to what one chooses to attend to, without compulsive redirection.

Free response: the capacity to respond to situations from internal values rather than conditioned triggers.

Autonomic sovereignty: the regulatory self-possession of the organism — its capacity to maintain internal coherence across perturbation.

Relational trust: the condition of being genuinely open to another person, based on sustained mutual presence rather than optimized social performance.

Coherent awareness: the state of organized, flexible, internally regulated consciousness from which all other capacities emerge.

Embodied connection: the physiologically grounded experience of genuine human relationship, involving synchronized biological coupling and mutual autonomic co-regulation.

None of these can be patented. All of them can be degraded by the patentable perimeter.

Why Measurement Does Not Equal Ownership

One of the most important confusions generated by the extraction economy is the conflation of measurement with ownership.

The argument runs: if I can measure it, I have a kind of property claim on the measurement. If I have a property claim on the measurement, I can do with it as I choose. If the subject produced the signal that I measured, then the subject is the source of the data and is owed something — perhaps notice, perhaps consent, perhaps compensation — but I own the measurement.

This argument has been extraordinarily successful at structuring data law and data practice. Notice-and-consent regimes accept it implicitly: the data belongs to the collector once consent has been obtained. The subject has provided notice that collection will occur, consented to it, and thereafter has no further claim on what is done with the collected data.

But measurement does not constitute ownership in the ethically relevant sense. Consider an analogy.

A physician can measure a patient's blood pressure, heart rate, and respiratory rate. The physician's measurement is real, accurate, and informative. The measurement does not give the physician any claim over the physiological states that produced it. The patient's cardiovascular system remains the patient's. The physician may use the measurement to inform treatment. The physician may not use it to modify the patient's physiological state without consent. The physician may not sell it to insurers who will use it to optimize premium extraction. The physician may not use it to build a behavioral profile for commercial exploitation.

Medical law embodies this intuition: the measurement is a tool for the patient's benefit. It does not transfer a property interest in the patient's body to the measurer.

The extraction economy has attempted to establish a different principle: that commercial measurement generates commercial property rights. That a camera measuring my pulse creates a data asset belonging to the camera operator. That a Wi-Fi router measuring my presence in my home creates a location dataset belonging to the router manufacturer. That an application measuring my emotional engagement with content creates a behavioral profile belonging to the application developer.

This principle is ethically wrong. The measured phenomenon — the body, its state, its behavior, its relationships — remains the subject's. The measurement is derived from the subject. The derivative does not supersede the source. The subject did not consent to having their physiological state converted into a commercially tradeable asset by virtue of being present in a room with a Wi-Fi router. The subject's presence is not consent. The subject's body is not an offer.

Why Influence Does Not Equal Consent

The second major confusion generated by the extraction economy involves influence and consent.

The argument runs: the system influenced the subject's behavior, and the subject behaved in a way that generated commercial value. The subject's behavior constitutes a kind of revealed preference — a demonstration of what the subject actually values and chooses. The system merely provided the conditions under which the preference was revealed.

This argument is wrong for a reason that goes to the foundation of consent theory.

Genuine consent requires three conditions: adequate information, absence of coercion, and decision-making capacity. The optimization loop systematically undermines all three.

Adequate information: the subject cannot meaningfully consent to optimization they cannot perceive, describe, or audit. A person who clicks "I agree" to a terms-of-service agreement has provided formal consent but not meaningful consent — they do not know what they are agreeing to, and the mechanism of influence is designed to be imperceptible.

Absence of coercion: influence becomes coercion when it exploits biological vulnerabilities that the subject cannot effectively defend against. Variable reward schedules exploit the dopaminergic reward system in ways that are not meaningfully distinguishable from other addictive conditioning mechanisms. Arousal-based attention capture exploits the orienting response — a hard-wired, involuntary, evolutionarily conserved mechanism for directing attention toward novel or threatening stimuli. Social comparison threats exploit the brain's evolved priority processing for status and belonging. These are not preferences. They are biological vulnerabilities. Systematically exploiting them at scale does not constitute obtaining consent. It constitutes leveraging compulsion.

Decision-making capacity: the optimization loop preferentially engages people when their decision-making capacity is reduced — when they are tired, stressed, emotionally aroused, or cognitively loaded. Studies of consumer decision-making consistently demonstrate that deliberate, reflective decision-making capacity is reduced under conditions of high arousal, time pressure, and cognitive load. The optimization loop provides all three. Decisions made under these conditions do not represent the subject's reflective preferences. They represent the product of the loop's targeted degradation of the subject's decision-making capacity at the moment of decision.

Influence under these conditions is not the revelation of preference. It is the manufacture of compliance.

Why Prediction Does Not Equal Understanding

The third major confusion is between prediction and understanding.

The optimization economy produces extraordinary predictive capability. Modern behavioral prediction systems can forecast individual behavior with accuracy that exceeds naive expectation across many domains. This predictive accuracy is sometimes presented as evidence of understanding: if the system can predict what a person will do, it understands something important about that person.

This conflation is wrong, and the reason it is wrong matters for the ethical argument.

Prediction requires identifying statistical regularities between inputs and outputs. Understanding requires grasping the causal, experiential, and intentional structure from which those regularities arise. A model can achieve high predictive accuracy by exploiting statistical patterns that have no correspondence to the causal or experiential structure of the phenomenon it predicts.

A classic example from statistics: a model trained on historical data could predict the behavior of a random number generator with perfect accuracy if the "random" generator had a hardware flaw that produced a detectable pattern. The model would predict correctly but would understand nothing about randomness, computation, or the nature of the system it was predicting. Its predictive success would be entirely an artifact of the pattern, which has no relevance to the causal structure of the system.

Analogously: a behavioral prediction system that achieves high predictive accuracy by exploiting biological vulnerabilities — arousal response patterns, variable reward sensitivities, status threat reactions — does not thereby understand the person it predicts. It has identified statistical regularities between environmental perturbations and behavioral outputs that are mediated by those vulnerabilities. The regularities are real. The understanding is absent.

This distinction matters ethically because the claim to understand a person — to know what they value, who they are, what they need — is the claim that justifies intervention in their lives. A physician who understands a patient's condition is authorized to recommend treatment. A therapist who understands a client's psychological structure is authorized to guide intervention. The authorization derives from the understanding.

The optimization system claims, implicitly, the authority to intervene in the subject's life — to select, deliver, and time perturbations designed to produce specific states — on the basis of a predictive relationship that involves no understanding. The authority is not earned. It is appropriated.

The Ethical Asymmetry: A Full Account

The ethical asymmetry at the center of this paper can now be stated completely.

The patentable perimeter encompasses all the technical capabilities by which the organism can be sensed, inferred, modeled, perturbed, and conditioned: cameras, spectrometers, Wi-Fi sensing systems, acoustic arrays, lighting modulation systems, interface design methods, behavioral prediction models, digital twins, and intervention engines. Each component fits within the legal machinery of technical invention. Each is individually patentable. Their collective enclosure of the human organism is legally protected by the sum of their individual patents.

The non-patentable interior encompasses all the human states, capacities, and conditions that make life meaningful and that cannot be legitimately enclosed as property: conscious presence, interior meaning, lived identity, relational trust, empathic attunement, autonomic sovereignty, uncoerced attention, free response, and the coherent awareness from which all of these emerge.

The asymmetry is this: the machinery that surrounds, measures, models, perturbs, and conditions the interior is legally protected. The interior it surrounds is not adequately legally protected. Patent law protects the perimeter. Privacy law offers inadequate protection for the interior. And no existing legal framework directly addresses the specific harm of having the conditions of coherent inner life systematically degraded by technically sophisticated, individually legitimate, but collectively enclosing systems.

This is not an asymmetry that can be resolved by better privacy law alone. Privacy law addresses the collection, use, and disclosure of personal information. The harm described in this paper is not primarily informational. It is physiological, attentional, relational, and existential. The organism's conditions for coherent self-regulation are degraded — not because information was collected — but because the environment was systematically perturbed to make the organism more predictable and less coherent.

The legal response must therefore be built on a different foundation.

PART VI — THE LAW

32

Why Privacy Is Not Enough

Privacy law, as currently constituted, addresses the wrong level of the problem.

The dominant privacy law paradigm — embodied in GDPR, CCPA, BIPA, HIPAA, and related frameworks — focuses on personal information: its collection, storage, processing, use, disclosure, and the rights of the subject with respect to these activities. The core obligations are notice and consent: inform the subject what is collected, obtain consent before collection, and honor the subject's rights to access, correction, and deletion.

This paradigm assumes that the harm of privacy violation is informational: that personal information, improperly handled, exposes the subject to reputational, economic, or dignitary harm through its disclosure to third parties who use it to disadvantage the subject.

That assumption is increasingly incorrect.

The harms described in this paper do not require disclosure. They do not require misuse of collected data in the conventional sense. They arise from the use of collected data to guide real-time environmental perturbation that degrades the subject's physiological, attentional, and relational state.

A person whose rPPG-extracted pulse data is used to adjust the timing and content of their content feed is harmed by the perturbation, not by the disclosure. The pulse data was never shared with a third party. It was used internally to tune the intervention loop. No conventional privacy violation occurred.

A person whose Wi-Fi-inferred presence in their home is used to adjust the lighting, temperature, and sound environment to influence their mood and alertness is affected by the actuation, not by the data disclosure. The location data was never shared. The environmental change was never described as a manipulation. No conventional privacy violation occurred.

A person whose digital twin was used to test the effectiveness of a stress-inducing content sequence before that sequence was deployed to their real feed is harmed by the conditioning, not by the disclosure. The twin model was never shared. The consent form described "personalization." No conventional privacy violation occurred.

Privacy law cannot reach these harms because they are not informational. They are physiological and behavioral. The subject's body was the target of optimization, not the subject's personal information. The harm occurred inside the body, not in a database.

A legal framework adequate to the patentable perimeter must address the non-informational harms: the physiological manipulation, the autonomic disruption, the attentional conditioning, and the relational degradation.

Consent in a Perimeter Architecture

The notice-and-consent regime also fails because consent becomes meaningless in a perimeter architecture.

Notice requires that the subject be informed of what is collected. In a perimeter architecture, the subject cannot be adequately informed because:

The collection is pervasive and multimodal: informing a subject of all the channels through which their physiological state is being sensed — camera-based rPPG, Wi-Fi CSI motion detection, acoustic analysis, atmospheric VOC detection, device interaction timing — would require a disclosure document of extraordinary length and technical complexity. The complexity defeats comprehension. A consent to a legally adequate notice that is practically incomprehensible is not meaningful consent.

The inference is opaque: the subject can be informed that physiological signals are collected. They cannot be meaningfully informed of what is inferred from those signals, because the inferences are produced by complex machine learning models whose outputs are not predictable even to their developers in specific instances.

The perturbation is imperceptible: the subject cannot consent to specific perturbations because those perturbations are selected in real time, are designed to be imperceptible, and are delivered through channels — content feeds, lighting, notification timing — that the subject does not experience as perturbation systems.

The environment itself is the medium: when the environment is the interface, there is no clear separation between the perturbed environment and the unperturbed background against which consent could be meaningfully given. Consenting to enter a building should not constitute consent to atmospheric sensing, lighting modulation, and behavioral conditioning.

The consent architecture must therefore be restructured. The current model — burden on the subject to read and consent to disclosures designed by the collector — must be replaced by a model that begins from the premise of protection and requires affirmative justification for each category of physiological intervention.

The Digital Battery Tort

The most important new legal concept required by the patentable perimeter is the digital battery tort.

Battery, in tort law, is the non-consensual, harmful or offensive touching of another person. The key elements are: physical contact, non-consent, and harm or offense. Battery does not require intent to harm. It requires only intent to make the contact, combined with the contact's non-consensual character.

The doctrinal innovation required here is the extension of battery to non-contact physiological intrusion.

The patentable perimeter, when operating its intervention loop, makes contact with the subject's physiological system. The contact occurs through the perturbation channels: light, sound, content, notifications, environmental modulation. The contact produces physiological effects: changes in arousal, attention, mood, autonomic state, hormonal levels. The contact is intentional in the relevant sense: the perturbation was selected by an optimization system precisely because it was predicted to produce a physiological effect.

The contact is non-consensual in the relevant sense: the subject did not consent to having their autonomic nervous system targeted by an optimization system. Clicking "I agree" to a terms-of-service agreement that does not describe the physiological targeting that will occur is not consent to that targeting.

The harm is real: degraded autonomic regulation, disrupted sleep, reduced emotional self-regulation capacity, increased inflammatory markers, reduced cognitive flexibility, and in some cases clinical-level anxiety, depression, and addiction-spectrum behavioral patterns.

The digital battery tort would be defined as follows:

Digital battery occurs when a person or entity intentionally deploys a system that (1) measures or infers the physiological or psychological state of an individual without that individual's meaningful consent, (2) uses that measurement to select and deliver environmental perturbations designed to alter the individual's physiological or psychological state, and (3) produces physiological or psychological harm to the individual as a result.

The elements:

Intentional deployment: the actor intentionally operates the system. Intent to harm is not required. Intent to operate the optimization loop is sufficient.

Physiological measurement without meaningful consent: "meaningful consent" requires that the subject was informed in understandable terms of the specific physiological effects being targeted, not merely the data types being collected.

State-targeted environmental perturbation: the perturbation must have been selected based on the measured or inferred physiological state, not merely delivered to all users uniformly. Personalized physiological targeting is the distinguishing criterion.

Physiological or psychological harm: harm includes measurable autonomic dysregulation, sleep disruption, anxiety disorders, depression, addiction-spectrum behavioral patterns, and relationship disruption attributable to platform-induced attentional fragmentation.

The digital battery tort would be available as both a private cause of action and a basis for regulatory enforcement. It would not require proof of intent to harm — only proof that the perimeter was deliberately operated, that it targeted the plaintiff's physiological state, and that it produced measurable harm.

The concept of "digital battery" matters because it places the harm where it actually occurs: in the subject's body. Privacy law places the harm in the data. Battery places the harm in the organism. The organism is the correct location.

Sensory Integrity as a Protected Interest

The first component of the new rights framework is sensory integrity.

Sensory integrity is the right of an individual to be free from non-consensual environmental perturbation designed to influence their physiology, mood, attention, or behavior.

This right is analogous to the right to bodily integrity — the well-established right to refuse unwanted physical contact, including medical treatment. Bodily integrity protects the physical boundary of the body. Sensory integrity protects the perceptual and regulatory boundary — the sensory field through which the organism receives environmental information and maintains its regulatory relationship with the surrounding world.

The sensory field is part of the organism's regulatory environment. The autonomic nervous system continuously monitors environmental inputs and adjusts physiological parameters accordingly. Lighting, sound, temperature, chemical composition of the air, and the social and digital stimuli delivered through screens — all of these are inputs to the organism's regulatory system. A system that deliberately manipulates these inputs to produce specific physiological outputs is intervening in the organism's regulatory process without consent.

Sensory integrity protection would impose the following requirements:

Disclosure of intended physiological effects: any system designed to influence physiology, mood, attention, or arousal through sensory channels must disclose its intended physiological targets in understandable terms — not merely its data collection practices.

Prohibition on non-consensual state-targeted perturbation: perturbations selected on the basis of individually measured or inferred physiological state, deployed without specific consent to physiological targeting, would constitute a violation of sensory integrity.

Right to audit: individuals would have the right to understand, in general terms, what physiological targets have been associated with the systems they interact with, and what perturbations have been delivered to them on that basis.

Default protection for identified high-risk spaces: homes, schools, healthcare settings, and other environments where autonomic vulnerability is elevated would receive heightened protection by default.

Autonomic Integrity as a Protected Interest

Autonomic integrity is the right of an individual to be free from systems that intentionally exploit or degrade the body's self-regulatory capacity.

This right is narrower and more specific than sensory integrity. It targets systems that specifically exploit identified biological vulnerabilities — arousal pathways, reward circuits, status-threat systems, social exclusion responses — to produce predictable behavioral outputs.

The distinction between permissible influence and autonomic integrity violation is the distinction between engagement and exploitation.

Permissible influence includes: presenting information, making arguments, offering opportunities, providing feedback, creating aesthetically or intellectually engaging content, designing pleasurable or convenient experiences. These forms of influence work through the subject's cognitive faculties — their capacity for evaluation, deliberation, and choice.

Autonomic integrity violation includes: deliberately inducing arousal states that impair deliberate choice; exploiting variable reward schedules known to produce compulsive behavioral patterns; deploying social comparison and status threat mechanisms to produce fear-based behavioral compliance; timing perturbations to exploit identified windows of autonomic vulnerability; and accumulating individualized vulnerability maps for deployment against the subject.

The legal standard for autonomic integrity violation would be:

An autonomic integrity violation occurs when a person or entity intentionally deploys a system that (1) identifies specific biological vulnerabilities in an individual's arousal, reward, social comparison, or autonomic regulation systems, and (2) uses those identified vulnerabilities as the basis for delivering perturbations designed to produce behavioral compliance, without the individual's meaningful consent to autonomic targeting.

Biometric Quiet as a Protected Condition

Biometric quiet is the condition of being in a space or time where one's body is not continuously being converted into a data surface.

Unlike the rights frameworks above, biometric quiet is not primarily a right against specific actors. It is a condition — an environmental property — that the law must affirmatively create by regulating which spaces, systems, and devices can engage in biometric monitoring.

The principle underlying biometric quiet is this: some human states require freedom from measurement in order to fully exist.

This is not merely a philosophical claim. It has a physiological basis. The awareness of being observed produces measurable physiological effects — the social evaluative threat response — that alter the subject's autonomic state, behavioral pattern, and experiential quality. A person who knows they are being physiologically monitored, or who is in an environment where such monitoring occurs invisibly, cannot fully enter the states that measurement disrupts. The measurement itself changes what is being measured.

This is particularly significant for states of vulnerability, intimacy, grief, and genuine connection — states that require the organism to be in an open, undefended, low-threat-appraisal mode that is incompatible with the social evaluative threat response produced by perceived monitoring.

Biometric quiet zones would include, as a minimum:

Primary residences: the home is the most fundamental refuge of the organism. Continuous physiological monitoring in the home, without explicit consent to each monitoring system's specific physiological targets, should be prohibited.

Educational settings for minors: children's physiological and behavioral patterns should not be the basis for commercial data extraction. The classroom should be a protected space.

Healthcare settings: the vulnerability inherent in healthcare encounters makes physiological monitoring in healthcare settings particularly ethically fraught when it serves commercial rather than clinical purposes.

Therapeutic and pastoral settings: spaces of psychological, pastoral, or spiritual care require the freedom from monitoring that genuine opening to another requires.

Grief, ritual, and memorial spaces: the physiological states associated with grief, mourning, and spiritual practice are particularly vulnerable to exploitation and require explicit protection.

The concept of biometric quiet is stronger than privacy because it begins from the premise that some spaces simply should not be instrumented, regardless of consent. It is an environmental right rather than an informational right.

Anti-Conditioning Rights

Anti-conditioning rights protect against the individualized deployment of a subject's own vulnerability profile against them.

The distinction from sensory integrity and autonomic integrity is temporal. Sensory and autonomic integrity protect against real-time perturbation. Anti-conditioning rights protect against the accumulation and use of individualized vulnerability maps over time.

An individualized vulnerability map is built when an optimization system accumulates sufficient data about a specific individual to identify:

  • The specific stimuli that reliably produce arousal in this individual.
  • The specific social comparison triggers that reliably activate status threat responses in this individual.
  • The specific emotional content that reliably produces reward-seeking behavior in this individual.
  • The specific times of day and physiological states in which this individual is most susceptible to each type of trigger.
  • The specific framing, timing, and delivery channels that maximize the effectiveness of each trigger for this individual.

When this map is assembled and used to select and time future perturbations, the optimization system is not influencing a population. It is conditioning a specific individual using information about that individual's specific vulnerabilities that the individual has never meaningfully disclosed.

Anti-conditioning rights would impose:

Prohibition on individualized vulnerability mapping for commercial deployment: a system that identifies specific biological or psychological vulnerabilities in a specific individual and uses those vulnerabilities as the basis for future perturbation deployment would be subject to legal challenge.

Right to vulnerability map audit: individuals would have the right to understand, in general terms, what vulnerability-related inferences have been made about them by systems they interact with.

Consent requirement for adaptive behavioral targeting: any system that adapts its perturbation strategy based on individually measured response — rather than population-level averages — requires specific, informed consent for that adaptation.

Relational Sanctuaries

Relational sanctuaries are environments where human connection is not subject to interception, scoring, modeling, or optimization by extraction systems.

The justification for relational sanctuaries is the claim developed in Part IV: genuine human connection requires biological preconditions — autonomic regulation, attentional presence, emotional availability, mutual vulnerability — that are degraded by the perimeter's presence in the relational space.

This is not merely about privacy in the conventional sense. The problem is not that the content of a conversation might be overheard. The problem is that the presence of an optimization system in the relational space — even silently, even without explicit surveillance of the conversation — changes the physiological and psychological conditions of the relationship itself.

When a digital device is present in a relational encounter — phone on the table, smart speaker in the room, smart glasses on the face — the monitoring possibility alters the social evaluative threat landscape for both parties. The organisms sense (consciously or not) that their physiological and behavioral states may be sensed and recorded. This awareness — or even its subliminal possibility — shifts the autonomic balance toward mild sympathetic activation: enough to reduce the depth of parasympathetic engagement, enough to reduce the physiological synchrony quality of the encounter.

Relational sanctuaries would require:

Device-free protected relational spaces: legal protection for spaces designated as relational sanctuaries, within which the operation of biometric sensing systems is prohibited.

Default protection for intimate relational contexts: healthcare conversations, therapeutic encounters, intimate partnerships, family gatherings, and spaces designated for grief and ritual should receive relational sanctuary status by default, requiring explicit affirmative choice to permit monitoring.

Prohibition on relational data monetization: data derived from intimate relational contexts — even data derived from individual physiological signals in those contexts — should be prohibited from commercial exploitation on the basis of the relational context from which it was derived.

The Anti-Enclosure Doctrine

The five specific rights described above — sensory integrity, autonomic integrity, biometric quiet, anti-conditioning rights, and relational sanctuaries — are components of a broader legal principle that can be called the anti-enclosure doctrine.

The anti-enclosure doctrine holds that patent protection for technical systems does not confer authority to enclose the human conditions those systems target. The legal protection of a camera does not confer the right to use that camera to condition the behavior of the person it observes. The legal protection of a lighting system does not confer the right to use that system to manipulate the circadian rhythm of a person who has not consented to that manipulation. The legal protection of a digital twin does not confer the right to use that twin to test interventions on a person who does not know they have a twin.

The anti-enclosure doctrine distinguishes between two types of patent authority:

Authority over the technical system: the patent holder has the exclusive right to make, use, sell, and license the claimed system. This authority is legitimate and limited.

Authority over the human subject the system targets: no such authority is conferred by patent law. The patent protects the machine, not the human. The patent protects the method, not the person the method is applied to. The patent protects the invention, not the organism the invention senses, models, and perturbs.

The anti-enclosure doctrine would impose a categorical limit: no technical system may claim authority over the human organism by virtue of patent protection for its components. The legal protection of the perimeter does not extend to the interior.

PART VII — THE DEFENSE

Coherence Practices as Political Practice

The coherence defense described in Part IV is not merely a matter of individual wellbeing. It is a political practice.

The optimization economy depends on a specific physiological condition in its subjects: chronic sympathetic activation, fragmented attention, compulsive behavioral patterns, and emotional volatility. These conditions make subjects more predictable, more reactive to specific triggers, and more susceptible to the intervention loop. They are conditions the perimeter produces and the perimeter requires.

When individuals cultivate autonomic coherence — through breath practice, deliberate attention training, device-free relational engagement, sleep protection, and physical movement — they do not merely improve their personal wellbeing. They degrade the perimeter's optimization target. A coherent subject is harder to classify accurately, because coherence increases behavioral variability in the ways that matter for prediction resistance. A coherent subject is more resistant to arousal-based attention capture. A coherent subject is less susceptible to variable reward schedule manipulation. A coherent subject's physiological state is less reliably moved toward target states by standard perturbation sequences.

This is not a guarantee of imperviousness. The perimeter has been refined against extraordinarily diverse human subjects and is highly effective across the population distribution. Individual coherence practice is necessary but not sufficient as a defense. It must be accompanied by structural change.

But it is not nothing. The cultivation of coherence is simultaneously a personal health practice and a form of political resistance — a refusal to be the predicted subject the optimization economy requires.

Design Principles for Coherence-Supporting Systems

Technology need not be extractive. The same technical capabilities that power the perimeter can be deployed in service of human coherence.

Design principles for coherence-supporting systems include:

Physiological sovereignty: systems that display physiological data to the user for the user's benefit, under the user's control, without transmitting that data to third parties or using it to tune external optimization.

Coherence feedback: consumer-accessible HRV biofeedback systems that support the user's autonomic self-regulation without the data leaving the device.

Attention support: interface designs that support sustained, directed attention rather than fragmenting it — designs that include deliberate stopping points, absence of infinite scroll, reduced notification density, and explicit attention protection modes.

Relational presence design: devices and platforms designed to facilitate genuine human connection, including presence indicators that reflect the quality of engagement rather than its quantity.

Transparent perturbation: any system that adapts its outputs based on user physiological state should be required to disclose those adaptations to the user in real time — to make visible what the coherence-hostile perimeter makes invisible.

Default protection: all systems should default to the minimum collection and perturbation consistent with their stated function. Expansion beyond default requires explicit, specific, informed consent for each additional capability.

Policy Architecture

The legal and regulatory framework required to address the patentable perimeter is not simple. It requires changes across multiple domains of law simultaneously.

Patent law: the anti-enclosure doctrine requires a change in how courts and the USPTO evaluate patent applications for systems that target human physiological and behavioral states. Patent eligibility should not be denied solely on this basis — the technical innovation remains real — but the patent grant should not be understood as conferring authority over the human subject the system targets. This could be achieved through a required disclosure in patent applications for physiological targeting systems: a statement of what human physiological states are targeted, what effects on those states are intended, and what consent mechanisms are proposed.

Tort law: the digital battery tort requires either legislative creation or judicial extension of existing battery doctrine to non-contact physiological intrusion through perimeter systems. The elements outlined in Section 34 provide a workable foundation.

Consumer protection law: the FTC's authority over deceptive and unfair trade practices provides an existing hook for addressing the most egregious perimeter applications. Deploying a system designed to degrade user autonomic regulation as a means of increasing engagement — while marketing the platform as a social connection tool — is deceptive. The FTC's unfairness standard — conduct that causes substantial injury, that is not reasonably avoidable, and whose harm outweighs its benefit — applies directly to the optimization loop.

Building code and environmental law: biometric quiet zones require regulatory designation of protected spaces and prohibition on biometric monitoring in those spaces. This is analogous to existing regulations protecting other environmental conditions — air quality, noise levels, occupational exposure limits. Physiological monitoring density is an environmental quality that can and should be regulated.

Data protection law: existing data protection frameworks should be extended to explicitly cover inferred physiological and psychological states as a category requiring heightened protection, with specific restrictions on their use in real-time optimization systems.

Platform liability: intermediary liability frameworks should be reformed to impose direct liability on platforms that deploy optimization systems proven to produce population-level autonomic dysregulation, even absent proof of intent to harm.

What Comes Next

The argument of this paper points in two directions simultaneously.

Backward, toward the present: the patentable perimeter is already operational at scale. The capabilities described in Parts I through III are not theoretical futures. They are deployed systems, individually protected, collectively forming an enclosure around the human organism that no single piece of existing law adequately addresses. The harm is ongoing. The legal framework is inadequate. The doctrine required does not yet exist.

Forward, toward the future: the capabilities described in this paper will become more powerful, more integrated, and more pervasive. Millimeter-wave sensing chips are already shipping in consumer smartphones. Biosensor-integrated wearables are approaching medical-grade accuracy at consumer prices. The synthesis of large-language models with real-time physiological sensing and digital twin modeling represents a frontier that the current paper has only sketched. The synthetic signal problem will intensify as generative capabilities improve. The perimeter is not at its ceiling.

The window for adequate legal response is open but narrowing. The technical capability converges faster than legal doctrine develops. The commercial incentives for deployment outpace the institutional capacity for evaluation. The subjects of the perimeter — all of us, in every instrumentated environment — do not have decades to wait for doctrine to develop at its traditional pace.

The argument this paper makes is urgent because the harm it describes is ongoing. The ethical asymmetry — patent protection for the perimeter, legal silence about the interior — is not a future risk. It is the present condition.

The defense of the non-patentable interior requires, at minimum:

Recognition that the interior exists — that there is a domain of human experience, physiological state, and relational condition that cannot be legitimately enclosed by patentable machinery.

Recognition that the perimeter threatens the interior — that the assembled sensing, modeling, and conditioning capabilities described here have effects on coherent human life that are real, measurable, and harmful.

Recognition that existing law is inadequate — that privacy law, consumer protection law, and current tort doctrine do not reach the specific harms described.

And recognition that the stakes are not merely personal. The systematic degradation of autonomic coherence, attentional capacity, and relational presence — at population scale, over extended time periods, for commercial gain — is not a minor social inconvenience. It is an attack on the biological substrate of the conditions that make human civilization possible: the capacity to regulate our own states, to connect genuinely with others, to think clearly, to attend to what matters, and to respond freely from our own values rather than from externally imposed conditioning.

That is what is at stake.

That is what requires defense.

CONCLUSION

The public patent record and surrounding technical literature reveal a convergent technical perimeter around the human organism. This perimeter senses the body from multiple channels simultaneously. It infers internal states through multiple inference architectures simultaneously. It models individual subjects through continuously updated digital twins. It perturbs the environment through multiple actuation channels. And it learns — continuously, iteratively, individually — which perturbations most reliably move which subjects toward which target states.

The operative logic of this perimeter is adversarial optimization. Not adversarial in the sense of malice. Adversarial in the precise technical sense: optimizing against a target — the organism's predictability, the subject's compliance, the subject's engagement — by discovering the minimal environmental perturbations that produce the most reliable state changes. The smallest perturbation. The deepest effect.

The casualty of this optimization is coherence. Autonomic stability. Organized variability. The body's capacity to remain internally regulated while relating freely to another. The conditions under which genuine human connection, clear thinking, free response, and uncoerced attention become possible.

The ethical asymmetry is permanent and structural. The perimeter is patentable. The interior is not. The machinery of extraction — every camera, every inference model, every digital twin, every actuation system, every intervention engine — fits within the legal framework of technical invention. The coherent human organism those systems surround does not. The interior is formally outside patent law. But it is practically surrounded by patentable machinery, and current law offers it insufficient protection.

This paper has argued that the response must therefore move beyond privacy:

Beyond the notice-and-consent paradigm, toward a framework that acknowledges the non-informational nature of the harms.

Beyond individual data rights, toward the digital battery tort — the recognition of non-consensual physiological intrusion as a legally cognizable harm.

Beyond general data protection, toward specific rights of sensory integrity, autonomic integrity, biometric quiet, anti-conditioning protection, and relational sanctuary.

Beyond reactive regulatory enforcement, toward an anti-enclosure doctrine that limits the authority of patent protection to the technical system and refuses its extension to the human subject.

The technology that enables the perimeter is not inherently hostile to human flourishing. The same sensing capabilities that power the extraction architecture could power genuine health monitoring under the subject's control. The same modeling capabilities that enable the digital twin could enable deeply personalized healthcare under the subject's authority. The same environmental actuation capabilities that enable the conditioning loop could be designed to support autonomic coherence rather than exploit it. The question is not whether these capabilities exist. The question is who they serve, and under what authority, and toward what ends.

The non-patentable interior is not a passive victim. It is the source of everything the extraction economy ultimately requires: human attention, human behavior, human relationship, human meaning. The perimeter depends, ultimately, on the continued existence of the thing it is trying to convert into raw material.

The defense of the interior is therefore not only ethical. It is existential. And it begins with naming what cannot be owned.

The machine can patent the perimeter, but not the interior.

It can patent the sensor, but not the presence.

It can patent the model, but not the meaning.

It can patent the perturbation, but not the free response.

It can patent the intervention, but not the coherent awareness that intervention targets.

What cannot be patented cannot be owned.

What cannot be owned cannot be legitimately enclosed.

What cannot be legitimately enclosed must be defended.
